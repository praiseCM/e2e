01 / Study design & analytical samples
An observational, two-course, two-wave study with complementary session feedback. One instructor taught all four sections. Courses differed in semester, curriculum, composition, and schedule; there was no random assignment or non-AI comparison. The matched sample was 38 in G1 and 59 in G2. Complete pairs were 38/58 for familiarity, 38/59 for usefulness, and 38/58 for participation.
02 / Questionnaires & measurement wording
Three distinct single ordered items measured AI familiarity (1–4), potential usefulness (1–5), and participation potential (1–5). Familiarity referred to AI broadly. G1 usefulness wording shifted from improving understanding to course learning; G2 referred to construction-planning concepts. Temporal and cross-course measurement equivalence was not established. These items were analyzed separately, without constructing a composite learning score.
03 / Longitudinal matching & missing responses
Identifier formatting was standardized without inferring missing characters or using outcome similarity. G1 used unique exact matches and verified identifier-to-email links; only chronological one-to-one pairs were retained. G2 required a unique exact identifier at both waves and chronological order. Duplicate or unresolved candidates were excluded. Two different G2 respondents had one missing item; no values were imputed. Self-generated codes do not independently verify identity.
04 / Statistical analyses & baseline adjustment
Within-course mean POST-minus-PRE changes used paired t-based 95% confidence intervals. G1-minus-G2 observed-change differences used Welch–Satterthwaite intervals. Conditional POST models included course and numeric PRE, with HC3 standard errors and normal-reference 95% intervals. These are exploratory, marginal intervals; Holm-adjusted tests were also reported within three-indicator families. G2 sections were pooled. HC3 addresses heteroskedasticity and does not adjust for section clustering.
05 / Sensitivity, selection & secondary associations
Checks used categorical baseline coding, prior educational chatbot users, and the main G1 response window (37 rather than 38). The participation contrast was sensitive to one valid G1 record. Linked and unmatched PRE responses differed; legal-category worst-case bounds for unique-PRE records allowed either sign of mean change. POST-only Spearman associations between reported use and perceived learning/confidence included whole-record bootstrap and leave-one-out checks; they do not establish time ordering or dose response.
06 / Session feedback & topic indexing
All nonempty submissions were retained with item-specific denominators: 515 records across seven G1 forms and 1,119 across sixteen G2 forms, totaling 1,634 submissions across 23 forms. Topic labels came from dictionary-based descriptive indexing, with AI assistance during dictionary development. Categories overlap. There was no independent human double coding or interrater statistic. Coded summaries reproduce quantitative results; classification from original response text is not publicly available. Missing or nonspecific text does not mean that no uncertainty existed.
07 / Limitations & scope of claims
Optional AI use was encouraged but neither standardized nor measured as an individual treatment. Underlying model versions and verification accuracy were not recorded. Outcomes were self-reported, bounded single items, with selective matching and unavailable section labels. Session records may repeat respondents and were not linked to longitudinal records. G1 submission counts sometimes exceeded enrollment. These data do not demonstrate disciplinary performance, actual participation, causal effects, or population-wide learning gains.
A SHARED VOCABULARY

Read the evidence
with confidence.

Five concepts for interpreting the figures.

Longitudinal linkage
Connecting the same respondent’s PRE and POST records, allowing within-person comparisons. Session submissions were not linked this way.
Confidence interval
A range reflecting sampling uncertainty under the analysis assumptions. The displayed 95% intervals are marginal and exploratory, and do not account for section clustering.
Ceiling effect
Limited room for upward movement when responses start near the top of a bounded scale. A high starting distribution does not prove that a ceiling mechanism caused the observed pattern.
Observed change
The numerical POST response minus the PRE response for the same respondent. Differences in average change compare how much the two matched groups moved.
Baseline adjustment
A model comparing later responses while conditioning on the measured initial response. It answers a different question from observed change and does not remove all confounding or establish causality.

07RESEARCH INSIGHTS & IMPLICATIONS

Better questions.
More useful evidence.

Three interpretations for engineering educators, grounded in the study and bounded by its observational design.

01

Starting conditions matter.

Students may enter courses with different familiarity levels and expectations. Report those distributions and define the comparison question before interpreting change.

02

Experience is multidimensional.

Favorable ratings and unresolved technical questions can coexist. Pair appraisals with task-specific follow-up, such as explaining activity logic or justifying a resource assumption.

03

Evidence must match educational claims.

Perceptions, observed behavior, and disciplinary performance answer different questions. Assess explanations, calculations, schedules, or later unaided work when claiming competence.

These interpretations draw on alignment and validity perspectives; they do not establish causal mechanisms.
